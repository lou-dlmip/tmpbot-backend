## Overview

We developed El Niño risk analysis for our clients, starting with the Panama Canal. The canal depends on Gatun Lake, and when the lake runs low, the canal must limit how deeply loaded ships can be and how many can pass. We matched more than four decades of rainfall data with the lake's daily water levels since 1965 to measure how El Niño drives these shortages, then forecast lake levels for 2026–27 under weak, strong and super El Niño scenarios. We have since applied the same approach to power plants, mines, traded commodities and government budgets across Southeast Asia, South Asia and Latin America.

## Key results

During El Niño, Gatun Lake spent more than a third of days below its critical 84-foot level, against 7% during La Niña, and the 2023–24 El Niño brought the longest low-water spell since 1979, lasting more than a year. The El Niño signal reaches the lake about three months later, so clients can see the risk coming. In Southeast Asia, the same analysis traced El Niño to the power plants, coal mines and LNG terminals in its path, and on to the companies that own them and the governments that must respond.

## Lessons learned

El Niño is one of the few climate risks that can be seen coming months ahead, yet most risk analysis stops at the weather map. The value lies in connecting that warning to specific assets, owners, trade routes and budgets early enough to act. Checking the data matters as much as the model: missing lake readings recorded as zeros had hidden most of the El Niño signal until we found and removed them.
